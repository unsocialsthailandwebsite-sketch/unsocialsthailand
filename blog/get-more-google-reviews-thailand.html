How to Get More Google Reviews for Your Hotel in Thailand

Published August 2026 · Unsocials Thailand

One beach club in Krabi holds a 4.8-star rating across more than 3,500 Google reviews. For context, most successful venues on that coast sit somewhere between 200 and 600. It is not a marketing budget that produced the difference, and it is not the view — half of Krabi has a view like that.

It was a decision about when to ask.

In a destination market, your rating is your reputation

A guest choosing a hotel in their own city has context. They know the neighbourhood, they have friends who have been, they can drive past and look.

A visitor to Thailand has none of that. No local knowledge, no word of mouth, no way to tell a good street from a bad one. The star rating and the number beside it are doing the entire job of recommendation, and they are doing it before anyone has seen a single photograph of your property.

Volume matters as much as the average. A 4.8 across 3,500 reviews reads as a fact. A 4.9 across 40 reads as a family and their friends. Volume also stabilises you: at 3,500 reviews, one bad night cannot move the number. At 40, it can.

Why the standard approach fails

Almost every property in Thailand does the same thing. An automated email goes out two or three days after checkout, thanking the guest and asking for a review.

By then the guest is home. They are back at work, the tan is fading, and your request is sitting in an inbox alongside forty other things. The feeling that would have produced a warm review has already gone, and what is left is an obligation. Most people ignore it, and the ones who do not tend to write something flat.

The email is not the problem. The timing is.

Ask while they are still there

The Krabi venue asks at the moment the experience is peaking rather than after it has ended.

Somebody tags the venue in an Instagram story. Within the hour they get a genuine thank-you message — not a template — and a light ask: if you enjoyed it, a Google review would mean a lot.

That works for two reasons. The person has already publicly told everyone they are having a good time, so the decision is made. And their phone is already in their hand, which removes the only real friction in the whole process.

The same logic applies offline. The natural high point — the sunset, the last drink, the moment somebody says this was perfect — is when a member of staff should be asking, not three days later from an autoresponder.

Build it as a system, not a favour

The reason most venues get this wrong is that asking for reviews is treated as something you do when you remember. It needs to be somebody’s daily job.

  • Watch mentions every day. Tags, stories, check-ins. Every one is a warm lead for a review.
  • Reply personally, within the hour. A template reads as a template. Two specific sentences about their actual visit does not.
  • Make it one tap. Use your Google review short link. Put a QR code on the bill folder, the room card and the table tent. Nobody searches for your listing in order to review it.
  • Give staff a moment, not a script. One prompt at the natural high point of the visit works better than a policy nobody follows.
  • Reply to every review. It signals an active business to Google, and to the next person reading.

What Google allows — and what gets you punished

This is where properties get themselves into trouble, usually with good intentions.

Do not offer anything in exchange for a review. A free drink, a discount, entry into a prize draw — all of it breaches Google’s policies. Reviews obtained that way can be removed in bulk, and losing several hundred at once is considerably worse than never having had them.

Do not filter who you ask. Surveying guests first and sending the review link only to the happy ones is known as review gating, and it is also against Google’s policy. Ask everybody. A venue with a few three-star reviews among the fives looks real, and readers trust it more.

The advantage of the timing approach is that you need neither shortcut. You are asking people who have already, publicly, told you they were enjoying themselves.

Reviews are also how you rank on the map

This is the part most owners miss. Reviews are not only social proof — they are among the strongest inputs into local search ranking. Volume, average rating, how recently reviews arrived, and whether the business replies all feed into how prominently you appear when somebody searches beach club near me.

A steady flow beats a burst. Fifty reviews arriving across three months is worth more than fifty arriving in a week, and looks a great deal less suspicious.

Reply to the bad ones properly

Every venue gets a poor review eventually. What separates the businesses that recover from it is the reply.

The reply is not written for the person complaining. It is written for the several hundred people who will read that thread while deciding whether to book. A calm, specific, non-defensive response to an unfair review does more for you than the review costs you. An argument does the opposite, permanently.

What good looks like

For a mid-sized property in Thailand, a realistic target is five to fifteen new reviews a month, arriving consistently, with a reply on every one inside forty-eight hours. Hold that for a year and you have several hundred reviews and a rating a competitor will struggle to catch.

None of it requires a budget. It requires somebody to own it, and the discipline to ask at the right moment rather than the convenient one.

If your rooms are full and your rating is not moving, that is usually a process problem rather than a service one — and it is among the cheapest things in hospitality to fix. There is more on the commercial side in reducing OTA dependence for Pattaya hotels.
